Letters from Victorian London

October 23, 2026

Love-letter from a Gentleman to a Lady. The Temple, June 4th, circa 1870.

DEAREST,

  Days have passed by now since we have had the pleasure of a few moments’ conversation even; how these hours have dragged their slow pace along you and I alone can tell. It is only when we are left to the peaceful enjoyment of our own society that time flies. It may be that to-morrow at Mrs. E.’s we shall have a little time alone. We all dine there; she told me she should have a dance also, and that your mamma had promised her your sister and yourself should be of the party. May I ask for the first waltz? I send a few flowers, but I imagine you will only wear one, the rose in your hair; your sister is always pleased with a bouquet, so I shall not be very angry if you let her have them, only wear my rose.

  Your own
    EDWARD.

Invitation to a Friend. The Elms, August 6th, circa 1870.

MY DEAR ELEANOR,

  I should be very glad if you could come and spend a month with us; the country is really so lovely during hay––making time, and the evenings so deliciously cool, that such an admirer of nature, would, I think, enjoy the change from your wild, rocky scenery to our quiet peaceful valley, and from the roar of the waves to the music of murmuring brooks.
  I expect my brother and sister to spend the autumn with me, and if you can make up your mind to leave home for a short time, they, as well as I, will be delighted with your company. George, who is as merry and mischievous as ever, will, I know, do his utmost to add to your enjoyment, and make your visit a pleasant one.

  With kindest regards to all at home,
    Believe me,
      Your sincere friend,
        ANNIE LEIGHTON.

In 1840, Victorian London saw the introduction of the world’s first postal stamp: the Penny Black.

Priced at a single cent (USD 60c today), the Penny Black made sending letters cheaper and simpler. Before, letters were expensive, post-paid and billed by distance, which put it out of reach as a means of communication for most ordinary Victorians. The Penny Black changed that. All of a sudden, everyone could send letters to each other. Within a year, mail volume within the UK doubled.

Reading these letters has been quite fasinating. The first thing that stand out to me is the prose. Just look its length and elaborateness; the Victorians really knew how to stretch out a dinner invitation into a three-part novella. Who knew an invitation could be written so poetically?

The next thing I noticed, while reading the text, is just how different it feels. I’ve never written anything like that before. Maybe I could, but modern texting standards reserve prose of such length to things like confessions or breakup messages.

How are letters different from other forms of personal, remote communications?

Today, texting dominates, but the brevity of it doesn’t really allow for ideas to breathe. Emails exist, but 1. the spam problem makes it impossible for regular people to treat email as a network for serious writing, and 2. emails crucially can be edited. Letters, written on paper, could be edited, but it doesn’t quite have the same malleability as electronic text. In that sense letters much closer to a raw streams of thought than anything electronic.

And of course: letters can contain drawings and illustrations!

So if you have several minutes to spare today, I implore you to take a look through the Victorian Letters. In them you shall, I hope, experience the thoughtfulness and kindness in the lives of everyday Victorians. As for myself, I shall be writing more letters to my valued friends, whom I cherish very dearly much.

Yours, ever sincerely,
  -
